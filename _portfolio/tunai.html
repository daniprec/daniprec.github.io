Who needed the work and why

Tuna purse-seine fleets release floating objects with echosounder buoys attached. Each buoy sends acoustic readings by satellite every hour. The signal shows that something is under the buoy. It does not say how much tuna it is. Satlink, which makes the buoys, wanted a way to read the signal at scale. Fleet managers and researchers also use buoy data to study how tuna gather around floating objects.

My role and the collaboration

Tun-AI was a joint project between UCA Datalab at the University of Cádiz, Komorebi AI and Satlink, within my industrial doctorate. I worked with Komorebi from the university for a long time before Komorebi employed me in September 2022. Satlink provided the buoy data. The fleet association AGAC provided the logbook records of catches.

What I contributed

DashboardI built it, from start to finish. Satlink used it.
Data cleaningI did all of it, from raw buoy records to model-ready tables.
Machine learning modelsI assisted. My own estimate of my share is about 20%. Colleagues at Komorebi and Cádiz did most of the modelling.
PaperFirst author of the Fisheries Research paper. A follow-up study on how tuna gather around buoys (Marine Ecology Progress Series, 2023) used Tun-AI. I am the second author there.

Challenge and approach

The data came from about 70 million records from 16,419 buoys across the Atlantic, Indian and Pacific Oceans. Catch records were typed by hand and contained errors, so we cross-checked them against the buoy data. Fleets only set their nets when they expect a large school, so small catches are rare in the logbooks. To balance that, we added the deployment of new buoys, which we treated as zero tuna.

The model reads a 72-hour window of echosounder data. It adds the buoy position and satellite ocean variables such as temperature, currents and chlorophyll. Gradient boosting performed best across three tasks: a yes or no answer for more than 10 tonnes, a three-class answer, and an estimate in tonnes.

What was delivered

  • The Tun-AI pipeline, which turns raw buoy and ocean data into biomass estimates.
  • A dashboard for data analysis and model deployment, used by Satlink.
  • A peer-reviewed paper, and a follow-up analysis of how long tuna take to find and leave a buoy. In that study the median time to first colonise a buoy ranged from 25 to 43 days, depending on the ocean.

Results and limits

  • The best classifier reached an F1 score of 0.925 for more than 10 tonnes, with 6.03% of test cases wrong.
  • The best regression model had a mean absolute error of 8.54 tonnes across test events. The same task with plain regression gave 10.03 tonnes.
  • Errors differ by event type. On fishing sets, the error was about 18 tonnes. On buoy deployments, it was about 1 tonne.
  • The model tends to underestimate very large schools, above 100 tonnes. Accuracy was lower in the Atlantic than in the other oceans.
  • The catch recorded in the logbook is treated as the true amount under the buoy. By-catch is not included.
  • These figures come from my thesis chapter based on the paper. This is a published research model. It is not a fleet-wide operational system.
